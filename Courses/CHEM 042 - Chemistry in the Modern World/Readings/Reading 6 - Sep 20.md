#### Donor vs acceptor (do both molecules need N, O, F?)

Yes, both molecules need an N, O or F, but only one of them needs the H attached to it:

| Role | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Donor** (gives the H) | has an H **directly bonded** to N, O or F |
| **Acceptor** (receives the H) | has an N, O or F with a **lone pair** |

You need one donor and one acceptor.

- **Water + water:** each one is both donor and acceptor, so they H-bond ✅
- **Acetone + acetone:** acetone ((CH₃)₂C=O) has an O, but its H's are all on carbon, so there's no donor ❌
- **Acetone + water:** water donates its H to acetone's O ✅
- **Methane + water:** methane has no N, O or F, so it can't accept ❌

> [!warning] Trap
> A molecule that contains O isn't automatically a donor. The H has to be bonded **directly** to the N, O or F. In CH₃OH (methanol), only the H on the O counts; the three H's on the carbon don't.
> So a molecule can be an acceptor only. It can't H-bond with itself, but it can with a donor like water. That's why acetone and ether dissolve in water fairly well.

## Summary

Intermolecular forces are the forces between molecules, not the covalent bonds inside them: they're generally weaker, less directional, and act over longer ranges. The reading sorts them into classes by strength, directionality and range, and almost all of them are electrostatic underneath. From strongest to weakest: ion-ion (two charged species, can be strong as covalent, non-directional, long-ranged), ion-dipole (an ion and a polar molecule, depends on the ion's charge and the dipole moment), hydrogen bonds (permanent dipoles with some covalent character, only with N, O, F, most stable at 180°), dipole-dipole (increases with dipole moment, weakens rapidly with distance), charge-induced dipole (an ion pulls a non-polar molecule's electrons into a dipole, which depends on polarizability, so mass and surface area), and London dispersion (temporary uneven electrons, crazy weak, grows with surface area and mass). The practice question applies this to a pure sample like NH3, where stronger intermolecular forces hold a more condensed state together.
