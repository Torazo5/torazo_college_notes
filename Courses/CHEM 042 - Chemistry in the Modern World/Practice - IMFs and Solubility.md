# SOLUTIONS

## A

**1. CH₃F** — **dipole–dipole + London only. No hydrogen bonding.**
The H-bond rule needs an H **covalently bonded to** N, O or F. Here the H's are on **carbon**; the F has lone pairs but no H attached to it. So pure CH₃F can **accept** an H-bond from something else, but two CH₃F molecules cannot H-bond to each other. C–H does not H-bond.

**2. CH₃OH** — **hydrogen bonding + dipole–dipole + London.** The O–H gives both a donor (the H on O) and an acceptor (O's lone pairs). Always list all three; H-bonding never replaces the weaker ones, it stacks on top.

**3. CO₂** — **London dispersion only.** The two C=O bond dipoles are equal and 180° apart → they vector-cancel → the **molecule** is nonpolar. Polar bonds ≠ polar molecule. Geometry decides.

**4. CH₂Cl₂** — **dipole–dipole + London.** Tetrahedral with two different outer atoms → dipoles don't cancel → polar. No H on N/O/F, so no H-bonding.

**5. NH₃ has the stronger IMFs** (bp −33 °C vs −88 °C) **even though PH₃ is heavier.**
- N–H: Δχ = 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9 → polar bonds, and H is on N → **hydrogen bonding**.
- P–H: Δχ = 2.2 − 2.2 = **0** → the bonds are **nonpolar**; only a small dipole from the lone pair. **London + weak dipole–dipole, no H-bonding** (P is not N/O/F).
The mass argument (heavier → more electrons → more London) is real but it loses here, because H-bonding is a much bigger jump than the London gain.

**6. Acetone cannot H-bond with itself, but it can accept H-bonds from water.**
It has O lone pairs (**acceptor**) but no O–H (**no donor**). Two acetone molecules = acceptor + acceptor = nothing. Acetone + water = water donates its O–H to acetone's carbonyl O. This is exactly why acetone's boiling point is modest (56 °C) but its water solubility is total. Same logic for ethers and esters.

**7. HF** — **hydrogen bonding + dipole–dipole + London**, with **H-bonding dominant**. H directly on F, and F has lone pairs. Δχ = 1.8, the most polar common single bond.

**8. I₂** — **London dispersion only** (nonpolar diatomic). It is a solid because London forces scale with **polarizability**: iodine has a huge electron cloud that distorts easily, and the instantaneous dipoles it forms are large. Weakest *per interaction* ≠ weakest *in total*.

**9. No, they differ.**
- **BF₃**: trigonal planar, three equal dipoles at 120° → cancel → **nonpolar** → **London only**.
- **NF₃**: trigonal pyramidal (lone pair on N) → dipoles don't cancel → **polar** → **dipole–dipole + London**.
The lone pair is the whole difference. Neither H-bonds (no H at all).

**10.** (a) **ion–dipole** (b) **ion–induced dipole** (also written charge-induced dipole) (c) **dipole–induced dipole** (d) **ion–ion**, the strongest of all.

## B

**11.** Same formula → same mass → same number of electrons → the *only* variable left is **shape / surface contact area**.
- **n-pentane**: long straight chain, molecules lie alongside each other → **most contact points** → the most total London force → **36 °C**.
- **2-methylbutane**: one branch, slightly more compact → less contact → **28 °C**.
- **neopentane**: nearly spherical, minimum surface area → touches least → **9.5 °C**.
Rule: **long/straight > branched/compact** for London, at equal formula.

**12.** Both are polar. The difference is one hydrogen's address.
- **Ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH)**: H is on **O** → full **hydrogen-bonding network** (donor + acceptor) → **78 °C**.
- **Dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃)**: the O has lone pairs, but every H sits on **carbon** → **no donor** → **dipole–dipole + London only** → **−24 °C**.
This is the canonical "same formula, different properties" pair — and MS cannot tell them apart, since M is identical. You need IR (broad O–H at ~3300 cm⁻¹ in ethanol, absent in the ether) or NMR.

**13. 1-propanol (97 °C) > 2-propanol (82 °C) > methyl ethyl ether (7.4 °C).**
- Both alcohols H-bond; the ether cannot donate → the huge drop to 7.4 °C is the missing O–H.
- Between the two alcohols: same H-bonding, so it's decided by **shape** — 1-propanol is a straight chain (better London contact), 2-propanol's OH is on the middle carbon, making the molecule more compact and slightly shielding the OH. Same reasoning as Q11, just stacked on top of H-bonding.

**14.** The two properties are asking about **different pairs of molecules**.
- **Boiling point** is about **solute–solute** (butanol–butanol vs ether–ether). Butanol H-bonds to itself; diethyl ether cannot donate, so it has only dipole–dipole + London. → huge gap (118 vs 35 °C).
- **Water solubility** is about **solute–water**. Water is the H-bond **donor** in both cases: it donates to butanol's O **and** to the ether's O. Both are acceptors, and both carry a similar-size C₄ hydrophobic tail. → nearly identical modest solubility.
Takeaway: **being a donor matters for boiling point; being an acceptor is often enough for solubility.**

**15.** Same formula, same one OH, but **different shape of the hydrophobic part**.
- **1-Butanol**: an extended 4-carbon chain sticking out into the water, forcing water to reorganize around a long nonpolar surface → costly → only 7.7 g/100 mL.
- **tert-Butanol**: the same 4 carbons balled up compactly around the OH → **smaller exposed nonpolar surface area** per molecule → the water network is disturbed less → **miscible**.
Note this is the *opposite* direction from Q11: compact shape **hurts** boiling point (less London), but **helps** water solubility (less hydrophobic surface).

**16. n-butane is the −0.5 °C one; isobutane is −12 °C.** Straight chain → more surface contact → stronger total London → higher bp. Same rule as Q11.

**17.** These are **stereoisomers**, not constitutional isomers — same connectivity, different 3D arrangement. The difference is **net dipole**:
- **cis**: both Cl's on the same side → their bond dipoles **add** → **polar** → gets **dipole–dipole** on top of London → **60 °C**.
- **trans**: Cl's opposite → dipoles **cancel** → **nonpolar** → **London only** → **48 °C**.
Same atoms, same masses, same polarizability — the entire 12-degree gap is the dipole–dipole term.

**18. IMF/boiling-point data: yes. MS alone: no.**
Isomers have identical molecular mass, so the M peak is the same (fragmentation patterns may differ, but M does not). Boiling point, viscosity and solubility all differ because they depend on IMFs, which depend on connectivity. To identify structure you use IR and ¹H NMR.

## C

**19. CH₄ (−161, London) < HCl (−85, dipole–dipole) < NH₃ (−33, H-bond) < CH₃CH₂OH (78, H-bond) < H₂O (100, H-bond network).**
- CH₄ → nonpolar, tiny → London only.
- HCl → polar, no H on N/O/F.
- NH₃ → H-bonds, but only **one** lone pair to accept with.
- Ethanol → H-bonds, and carries far more electrons/surface than NH₃ (London adds up).
- H₂O → **2 donors + 2 acceptors** per molecule → a full 3D network, the most H-bonds per molecule of anything on the list.

**20. The London exception.** London is weakest **per contact**, but it **accumulates** with size, surface area and electron count. Octane is a long C₈ chain with a huge polarizable surface and many contact points; HCl is a small molecule with one modest dipole. The ranking (H-bond > dipole–dipole > London) is only valid **comparing molecules of similar size**. Across very different sizes, London can win — same reason I₂ is a solid while HCl is a gas.

**21. Count the H-bonds per molecule, don't just rate their individual strength.**
- **HF**: 1 donor H, but 3 lone pairs — limited by having only **one** H to donate. It forms chains, not a network.
- **H₂O**: **2 donors + 2 acceptors**, perfectly matched → each molecule participates in up to **4** H-bonds → a continuous 3D network. Many medium bonds beat few strong ones.

**22. hexane (69) < hexanal (131) < 1-hexanol (157) < hexanoic acid (205).**
- **hexane**: nonpolar → London only.
- **hexanal**: polar C=O, acceptor only, no donor → dipole–dipole + London.
- **1-hexanol**: O–H → donor + acceptor → H-bonding.
- **hexanoic acid**: COOH → forms **two** H-bonds at once (cyclic dimer), effectively doubling the unit that must be vaporized.
The carbon skeleton is constant across all four, so the whole spread is the functional group's IMF.

**23. Acetone has the higher vapor pressure; water has the higher viscosity.** One rule covers both: **stronger IMFs → harder to escape the liquid (lower vapor pressure, higher bp) and harder to slide past neighbours (higher viscosity).** Water's H-bond network wins on both counts; acetone only has dipole–dipole.

**24. ion–ion > ion–dipole > hydrogen bond > dipole–dipole > ion–induced dipole > dipole–induced dipole > London.**
It misleads you when the molecules being compared are **very different in size**. For comparable sizes, trust the ranking; when one molecule is much larger/more polarizable, London's accumulation can overturn it (Q20).

## D

**25.**
| Solute | Water-soluble? | Solute–solvent force |
|---|---|---|
| (a) NaCl | **Yes** | **ion–dipole** (water's δ− O surrounds Na⁺, δ+ H's surround Cl⁻) |
| (b) hexane | **No** | only dipole–induced dipole / London — far too weak to pay for broken water H-bonds |
| (c) acetone | **Yes (miscible)** | water donates an H-bond to the carbonyl O (**acceptor-only is enough**) |
| (d) 1-octanol | **Barely** | H-bond at the OH, but an 8-carbon nonpolar tail dominates |
| (e) NH₃ | **Yes, very** | hydrogen bonding, both directions (donor and acceptor) |
| (f) I₂ | **No** | London / dipole–induced dipole only; dissolves in hexane instead |

**26.** Every alcohol is two things at once: a **polar H-bonding head (–OH)** and a **nonpolar hydrocarbon tail**. As the tail grows, the molecule's character shifts from head-dominated to tail-dominated. Short tails (C1–C3): the OH wins, fully miscible. C4: borderline, partly soluble. C8: the tail dominates and the molecule behaves like an alkane. Solubility follows the **ratio of polar to nonpolar surface**, not the mere presence of an OH.

**27.** Dissolving requires **breaking** water–water hydrogen bonds and oil–oil London contacts, and **making** water–oil contacts in their place. Water–oil can only be **dipole–induced dipole/London** — far weaker than the H-bonds that had to be given up. The books don't balance, so the system stays separated with water H-bonded to water and oil London-bonded to oil. Stating "oil is nonpolar" only names the input; the exchange of forces is the actual mechanism.

**28.** Ethanol is amphiphilic:
- **In water**: the **–OH head** hydrogen bonds with water (donor *and* acceptor) → fully miscible.
- **In hexane**: the **CH₃CH₂– tail** makes good London contacts with hexane's chain, and ethanol's own H-bond network is small enough (only a 2-carbon molecule, few H-bonds to break per molecule) that the cost is payable.
Small alcohols are the classic "dissolves in both" case — it's why ethanol works as a bridging solvent.

**29.** Acetone has a **carbonyl O with lone pairs** → it **accepts** hydrogen bonds from water, which is a strong solute–solvent interaction and repays the water–water H-bonds that are broken. Propane has no lone pairs and no dipole at all → the best it can offer water is dipole–induced dipole, which doesn't cover the cost. **A solute doesn't need to H-bond with itself to be water-soluble — it only needs to interact with water.**

**30.** The best force available between Na⁺/Cl⁻ and hexane is **ion–induced dipole** — the ion polarizes hexane's electron cloud. That must overcome the **ion–ion** attractions of the crystal lattice, which are the strongest IMF there is. Ion–induced dipole ≪ ion–ion, so the lattice stays intact. Water succeeds only because **ion–dipole** is the next-strongest force, not because water is "good at dissolving things."

**31. Dipole–induced dipole.** Water's permanent dipole polarizes the O₂ electron cloud, inducing a temporary dipole in it. The inducer does **not** have to be an ion — a permanent dipole works too, just more weakly. This force sits between ion–induced dipole and pure London. It's weak, which is why dissolved O₂ concentrations in water are small — but it's nonzero, which is why fish can breathe.

**32.** "…**comparable to or stronger than**…" — "like dissolves like."
For ethanol + water: the ethanol–water H-bonds formed are just as strong as the water–water and ethanol–ethanol H-bonds broken, so the exchange is even and the two mix in **all proportions** (miscible).

## E

**33. Polarizability** — how easily a molecule's electron cloud is distorted. Driven by:
- **number of electrons / molecular size (mass is a proxy, not the cause)**
- **surface area and shape** (straight chains touch more than compact ones)

The precision trap: London strength depends on the polarizability of the molecule — **any** molecule, polar or not. It is not "the surface area and mass of the **polar** molecule"; London acts between **every** pair of molecules, always, including two nonpolar ones. Polarity is what adds dipole–dipole **on top of** London; it isn't what makes London happen.

**34. False.**
Solid vs liquid at room temperature is set by **intermolecular forces**, not covalent bond strength. Covalent (intra-molecular) bonds are ~10–100× stronger than IMFs and are not broken during melting or boiling — only the IMFs are. H₂O and H₂S have comparable O–H/S–H bonds, yet water is liquid and H₂S is a gas, purely because of hydrogen bonding. **Phase change = overcoming IMFs; chemical reaction = breaking covalent bonds.**
