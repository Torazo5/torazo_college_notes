## Examples / Practice


## Summary

Higher-energy orbitals have more nodes, which are points with zero electron density. When two nuclei are close, their wavefunctions superpose. Constructive overlap puts electrons between the nuclei, so both nuclei get pulled together (a bonding MO, lower energy). Destructive overlap leaves a node between them, so nothing holds them together (antibonding, higher energy). Molecular orbitals are the wave shapes an electron can have around a whole molecule. The number of MOs equals the number of atomic orbitals combined, and they fill lowest-energy first like atomic orbitals. Bond order is (bonding − antibonding)/2: He₂ comes out to 0 and is unstable, while He₂⁺ is 0.5 and mildly stable. s orbitals only make sigma bonds (density on the axis), and p orbitals also make pi bonds (density around the axis). Unpaired electrons give $S$, and $2S+1$ tells you about reactivity. In NO, O is lower in energy because it has more protons, and the single electron in $\pi^{*}_{2p}$ decides where a UV excitation goes.
