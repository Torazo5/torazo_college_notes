# Quick Practice — Basics (30 min)

Every question gives you one reading and asks what the other instrument reads. Answer *bigger or smaller, earlier or later, and why* — no numbers needed. Say it out loud before opening the fold.

---

### 1. Two stopwatches on a muon

A muon is born high up and decays near the ground. The stopwatch riding it reads **2.2 μs** between birth and decay. The ground crew times the same flight with clocks at the top and bottom of the atmosphere.

Does the ground crew's number come out bigger or smaller than 2.2, and what physically is making the difference?

> [!success]- Answer
> Bigger. The ground crew always gets more.
>
> Reason, not rule: the muon's watch was *at* both events, so it's one clock reading a start and a stop. The ground crew can't do that — their two events happen at different places, so they need two separated clocks that had to be synchronised first. It's that synchronisation that their answer inherits, and it's exactly the thing the muon doesn't agree with. The single-clock reading is always the smallest one any frame can get.
>
> Sanity check on direction: muons reach the ground at all, so the ground must be measuring a longer life than 2.2 μs. If you ever get the direction backwards, ask "who used one clock?" — that one reads less.

### 2. Nose and tail

A rocket flies past you, nose first. The crew synchronised their nose and tail clocks on board, carefully. At one instant by *your* clocks, you photograph both.

Nose clock in the photo reads **12:00:00**. More or less than that on the tail clock?

> [!success]- Answer
> More — the tail reads a later time. Leading clocks lag; the one out front is the one that's behind.
>
> Why it isn't a crew error: the offset gets bigger the further apart the two clocks are, and bigger the faster the rocket goes. Nothing about their procedure does that. It's your "at one instant" slicing spacetime differently than theirs, and the further apart the clocks, the more the two slices diverge.
>
> Follow-up to test yourself: same rocket, half the length — is the gap bigger or smaller? (Smaller. Halve the separation, halve the offset.)

### 3. Both of you filming

I fly past you. You watch my clock through a telescope and it runs slow — my second takes longer than yours. I look back at yours.

Does my telescope show yours running slow, fast, or normal?

> [!success]- Answer
> Slow. By the same factor. Both of us, symmetrically.
>
> This isn't a contradiction because we're not doing the same comparison. When you say my clock is slow, you compared my *one* clock against *two* of yours at different places — and those two are only synchronised in your frame. I do the mirror: your one clock against two of mine, which you say aren't synchronised. Each of us is grading the other using a de-synchronised pair.
>
> The only way to force a single answer is to bring both clocks back to one place, and somebody has to turn around to do that. That turn is what breaks the tie — and that's the twin problem, not this one.

### 4. Tape measure on the rocket

The crew measures their rocket at **100 m** with a tape measure on board. You measure it as it flies past by marking where the nose and tail are at the same instant by your clocks.

Bigger or smaller than 100? And now the crew points their tape at *your* 100 m platform as they pass — what do they get?

> [!success]- Answer
> You get less than 100. They also get less than 100 for your platform. Same factor both ways.
>
> Not a contradiction for the same reason as Q3: your measurement depends on "nose and tail at the same instant," which is *your* simultaneity. The crew says your two marks weren't made at the same time at all — you marked the tail late, after the rocket had moved up, so of course you got a short answer.
>
> The reading that's special is the crew's own 100 m: the rocket isn't moving relative to their tape, so there's no simultaneity question in it at all. Every other frame reads less.

### 5. Sideways tape

Same rocket: **100 m** long, **10 m** wide, by the crew. You clock it past at high speed.

You get less than 100 for the length. What do you get for the width?

> [!success]- Answer
> Exactly 10. Widths don't change.
>
> The argument: paint a ring inside a tunnel at exactly 10 m across. If width contracted in your frame, you'd say the rocket sailed through clean and the crew would say it scraped — but whether there's paint on the hull is one event at one point, and *every* frame has to agree on that. So width can't be frame-dependent.
>
> Length dodges this because the disagreement there is about *when* you marked the ends, and "when" is allowed to differ. There's no "when" in a paint scrape.

### 6. Which flash first

Two lights flash: one on Mars, one on Earth. Your clocks say Mars flashed **3 minutes** before Earth. Light takes about 12 minutes to cross that gap.

Is there a frame where the Earth flash comes first? What if the gap between the flashes had been 20 minutes instead of 3?

> [!success]- Answer
> **3 minutes:** yes, some frame reverses them, and some frame calls them simultaneous. Light can't cross 12 minutes of distance in 3, so neither flash could have caused the other — nothing breaks when the order flips.
>
> **20 minutes:** no frame reverses them. Mars could have sent a signal that triggered Earth, so the order is locked in every frame. Relativity is allowed to scramble simultaneity; it's never allowed to let an effect precede its cause.
>
> The test is just: could light have made the trip in the time available? If no, order is up for grabs. If yes, it's fixed.

### 7. The rhino blades

The front blade's clock reads **0** and the rear blade's reads **−4.8/c** at the moment you start watching. The rule is "drop when your own clock hits 0," so the front blade drops right now and the rear one has to wait out its gap.

The rear blade's own clock will tick off 4.8/c before it drops. Does *your* watch tick off more or less than that while you wait?

> [!success]- Answer
> More. You wait longer than the blade's clock says.
>
> The two effects stack and you need both:
> 1. **Lag** tells you the rear clock is sitting at −4.8/c — that's how much waiting there is, in blade-clock ticks. Pure simultaneity, no dilation in it yet.
> 2. **Dilation** converts those blade ticks into your ticks. The blade's clock is the one running slow relative to you, so burning 4.8/c of its time costs you more than 4.8/c.
>
> Common wreck: treating the 4.8/c as already being your number and stopping there. It's a reading on a clock that's running slow, so it has to be stretched before it's yours.

### 8. Where it sticks

A pole runs through a barn. The farmer slams both doors shut at the instant the pole fits entirely inside, then reopens them — pole passes, undamaged. In the pole's frame the pole is *longer* than the barn, so it can't ever be inside.

If the pole never fit, and yet the pole also never got hit by a door — what did the doors do in the pole's story?

> [!success]- Answer
> They closed at different times. The far door shuts and reopens while the pole's nose is still short of it, then the near door shuts and reopens after the tail has cleared. Two separate door events, never boxed in.
>
> The farmer's "both at the same instant" is his simultaneity and the pole doesn't share it. Whether a door ever hit the pole is a real event, and both frames agree: no. Whether the pole was "inside at one moment" is a simultaneity claim, and they're allowed to disagree.
>
> General move: when a paradox feels like a contradiction, find the word *at the same time* and ask whose clocks. That's almost always the whole thing.

## Self-check

You're sound if, without writing a formula, you can:
- say which of two clocks reads less and point at *why* (one clock present at both events)
- say which of two separated clocks reads later on a moving object, and what makes the gap bigger
- give the direction of every effect before touching a number
- keep lag and dilation as two stacked steps, not one
