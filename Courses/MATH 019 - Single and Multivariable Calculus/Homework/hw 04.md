All second-order partial derivatives including mixed partials are: $f_{xx},f_{yy},f_{xy},f_{yx}$
First we can compute $f_{x}$ and $f_{y}$ because it'll be used for all of the following(done by simple trig differentiation identities):
$$
f_{x} = -y\sin(xy)
$$
$$
f_{y}=-x\sin(xy)
$$
Now the easier ones which is same variable second-order($f_{xx}$, $f_{xy}$):
$$
f_{xx}=-y\cos(xy)(y)= \boxed{-y^2\cos(xy)}
$$
$$
f_{yy}=-x\cos(xy)(x)=\boxed{-x^2\cos(xy)}
$$
(Through simple chain rule)

The last two are the harder two due to the product rule but can be done pretty easily regardless($f_{xy}$, $f_{yx}$):

$$
\boxed{f_{xy}=-\sin(xy)-xy\cos(xy)}
$$

$$
f_{yx}=-\sin(xy)+(-(\cos(xy)*xy))
$$
$$
\boxed{f_{yx}=-\sin(xy)-xy\cos(xy)}
$$
Here $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ are actually identical!

Using the limit definition of a partial derivative:
$$
f_{x}(a,b)=\lim_{ h \to 0 } \frac{f(a+h,b)-f(a,b)}{h}
$$
We can simply input for: $f_{x}(0,0)$ 

$$
f_{x}(0,0)=\lim_{ h \to 0 } \frac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}
$$
important note here is that the function that will be applied on $f(h,0)$ is different from $f(0,0)$ because at $(x,y)=(0,0)$, the output is just 0.

Thus the output is:
$$
f_{x}(0,0)=\lim_{ h \to 0 } \frac{0-0}{h} = 0
$$
Thus:
$$
\boxed{f_{x}(0,0) = 0}
$$

Similar can be done for $f_{y}$, however we would need our increment $h$ on the y value instead:
$$
f_{y}(a,b) = \lim_{ h \to 0 } \frac{f(a,b+h)-f(a,b)}{h}
$$

$$
f_{y}(0,0)=\lim_{ h \to 0 } \frac{f(0,h)-f(0,0)}{h}
$$
Similar idea for $f(0,0)$ is applied again and thus:
$$
f_{y}(0,0)=\lim_{ h \to 0 } \frac{0-0}{h} = 0
$$
And thus:
$$
\boxed{f_{y}(0,0) = 0}
